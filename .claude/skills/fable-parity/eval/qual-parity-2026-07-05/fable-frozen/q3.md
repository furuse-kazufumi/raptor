# q3 — fable-raw (archetype: hard-analysis)

## Task

Compare Fibonacci heaps vs pairing heaps as the priority queue for Dijkstra's algorithm. Give the worst-case and amortized costs of the relevant operations (insert, extract-min, decrease-key) for each, derive the resulting overall Dijkstra bound on a graph with V vertices and E edges, and state precisely WHERE each heap wins (sparse vs dense E, theory vs practice). Identify the single most subtle point in the amortized (potential-function) argument for decrease-key that is most often stated incorrectly, and state it correctly.

## Fable answer

# Fibonacci Heaps vs Pairing Heaps for Dijkstra

## 1. Per-operation costs (heap size n; for Dijkstra n ≤ V)

### Fibonacci heap (Fredman–Tarjan 1984/87)

| Operation | Amortized | Worst case (single op) |
|---|---|---|
| insert | O(1) | O(1) |
| find-min | O(1) | O(1) |
| decrease-key | **O(1)** | **Θ(n)** — a cascading-cut chain can be forced to linear length (Fibonacci heaps can be driven into linear chains, cf. CLRS Problem 19-4, with long runs of marked ancestors) |
| extract-min | O(log n) | Θ(n) — consolidation may touch Θ(n) roots |
| meld | O(1) | O(1) |

Potential function: Φ(H) = t(H) + 2·m(H), where t = number of root trees, m = number of marked nodes.

The size invariant that makes extract-min O(log n) amortized: a node of degree d roots a subtree of size ≥ F(d+2) ≥ φ^d, so max degree D(n) = O(log n) (base φ ≈ 1.618, i.e., log_φ n ≈ 1.44 lg n). This invariant holds *because* the mark/cascading-cut rule guarantees each node loses at most one child between the time it is linked under a parent and the time it is itself cut — hence the i-th child of any node has degree ≥ i − 2 (not i − 1, as in binomial heaps).

If per-operation worst-case bounds matter (real-time systems), the strict Fibonacci heap (Brodal–Lagogiannis–Tarjan 2012) and Brodal queues (1996) achieve the same bounds worst-case per operation, at substantial constant-factor cost.

### Pairing heap (Fredman–Sedgewick–Sleator–Tarjan 1986)

| Operation | Amortized | Worst case (single op) |
|---|---|---|
| insert | O(1) (Iacono 2000; original FSST analysis gave O(log n)) | O(1) |
| find-min | O(1) | O(1) |
| decrease-key | **open**: between **Ω(log log n)** (Fredman 1999 lower bound) and **O(log n)** (FSST); Pettie 2005 shows O(4^{√(log log n)}) = o(log^ε n) when insert/meld share that bound | **O(1) actual** (detach subtree, link with root) |
| extract-min | O(log n) | Θ(n) — two-pass pairing over up to n children |
| meld | O(1)–O(log n) amortized depending on analysis | O(1) |

Note the exact inversion of the trap for decrease-key: Fibonacci is O(1) *amortized* but Θ(n) worst-case single-op; pairing is O(1) *worst-case actual* but provably **not** O(1) amortized — the cheap cut leaves structural damage (loss of "heap-ordered tournament" information) that later extract-mins must pay for. FSST conjectured O(1) amortized; Fredman disproved it in a model that captures standard pairing heaps and their generalizations.

## 2. Dijkstra bound derivation

Operation counts: exactly V inserts (or ≤ V with lazy insertion), V extract-mins, ≤ E decrease-keys (at most one per edge relaxation, ≤ 1 per directed edge).

Because amortized costs telescope over any operation sequence starting from an empty heap, the summed amortized costs are a **genuine worst-case bound on total time** — a point that matters: no "the input might hit the Θ(n) op repeatedly" objection applies to the total.

- **Fibonacci:** V·O(1) + E·O(1) + V·O(log V) = **O(E + V log V)**. This is optimal for comparison-based Dijkstra with decrease-key. (For integer weights, RAM algorithms do better — Thorup, Fredman–Willard — but that's outside both heaps.)
- **Pairing (proven bound):** V·O(1) + E·O(log V) + V·O(log V) = **O((E + V) log V) = O(E log V)** for connected graphs. With Pettie's analysis: **O(E·4^{√(log log V)} + V log V)**.
- Fredman's Ω(log log n) amortized lower bound implies no analysis of the standard pairing heap can ever deliver O(E + V log V); the best conceivable is roughly O(E log log V + V log V). (Careful caveat: the lower bound is over adversarial operation sequences in Fredman's model; it rules out an O(1)-amortized-decrease-key *analysis*, which is what the Dijkstra derivation would need.)

## 3. Where each wins — precisely

**Theory (asymptotics):**
- E = O(V) (sparse): both are Θ(V log V). Fibonacci buys nothing — even a binary heap's O(E log V) = O(V log V) already ties.
- E = ω(V): Fibonacci strictly wins under the proven pairing bound. E.g. E = Θ(V log V): O(V log V) vs O(V log² V). E = Θ(V²) (dense): O(V²) vs O(V² log V) — and even against the Fredman-optimal hypothetical, O(V²) vs Ω(V² log log V).
- Crossover rule of thumb: Fibonacci's advantage is exactly the regime where the E·(decrease-key cost) term dominates V log V, i.e., E/V = ω(1). The denser the graph, the bigger the win.
- Degenerate dense case: for E = Θ(V²), the trivial array + linear-scan Dijkstra is also O(V²), matching Fibonacci with far smaller constants.

**Practice:** pairing heaps win almost universally.
- Fibonacci node: 4 pointers (parent, child, left, right sibling) + degree + mark bit; cascading-cut and consolidation bookkeeping; poor cache locality; constants typically 2–4× worse. Pairing node: 2–3 pointers (left-child/right-sibling + prev/parent), trivially simple code.
- Empirical studies (Stasko–Vitter 1987; Moret–Shapiro 1991; Larkin–Sen–Tarjan 2014 "back-to-basics") consistently rank pairing heaps at or near the top among decrease-key-supporting heaps and Fibonacci heaps well behind; implicit binary/4-ary heaps win outright when decrease-key is rare or eliminated.
- Structural reason the theoretical gap rarely bites: real Dijkstra runs execute far fewer than E decrease-keys (on random graphs the expected count is O(V log(E/V)), and on road networks it is tiny), so the E-term Fibonacci optimizes is mostly hypothetical. Many production codes skip decrease-key entirely (lazy deletion: re-insert and discard stale pops), giving O(E log E) with an implicit heap — usually the fastest of all in practice.
- Net: use Fibonacci heaps to *state* the O(E + V log V) bound; use pairing heaps (or a 4-ary implicit heap / lazy deletion) to *run* Dijkstra.

## 4. The single most subtle point in the decrease-key potential argument — and its correct statement

**The common incorrect statement:** "The marked nodes' potential pays for the cascading cuts, so Φ = (#trees) + (#marked nodes) gives O(1) amortized decrease-key."

**Why it's wrong:** with coefficient 1 on marks the argument collapses. A decrease-key performing c cascading cuts does Θ(c + 1) actual work, creates c + 1 new roots (Δt = +(c+1)), unmarks c nodes, and marks at most one node (the first unmarked non-root ancestor where the cascade stops — the source of the "+1"). Under Φ = t + m, ΔΦ = (c+1) + (−c + 1) = +2: the potential released by unmarking is *exactly cancelled* by the tree-potential increase from the new roots, the amortized cost is Θ(c), and O(1) fails.

**The correct statement:** each unit of mark potential must pay for **two** distinct debts, which is why the potential is Φ = t + **2**m. When a marked node is cascade-cut, its released 2 units cover (i) the O(1) actual work of performing that cut, **and** (ii) the +1 increase in tree potential from the root it just became — i.e., it prepays that root's future handling in extract-min's consolidation. Then ΔΦ = (c+1) + 2(−c + 1) = 3 − c, and amortized cost ≤ Θ(c+1) + (3 − c) (after scaling the potential unit to the cut cost) = O(1). The decrease-key analysis is thus not self-contained: its O(1) is an accounting transfer that keeps extract-min's O(log V) honest — and the mark-after-two-losses rule is simultaneously what preserves size(x) ≥ F(d+2), so cutting eagerly on the first child loss (no marks) breaks the analysis (every cut would cascade to the root with nothing to pay for it), while never cutting breaks the Θ(log n) degree bound that extract-min needs.

Two adjacent traps worth naming because they are stated wrong nearly as often: (a) "Fibonacci decrease-key is O(1)" without the word *amortized* — a single call can cost Θ(n); the guarantee is only on totals over a sequence (which is exactly why the Dijkstra total bound is still worst-case valid); (b) the mirror-image error for pairing heaps — their decrease-key really is O(1) *per call*, yet no potential function can make it O(1) *amortized* (Fredman's Ω(log log n)), because the O(1) cut increases potential rather than decreasing it.
